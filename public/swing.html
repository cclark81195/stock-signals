Research only. This page tests multi-day trades (hold for days, not minutes) on up to 10 years of daily prices for every S&P 500 stock, with trading costs included. Long trades are far fewer and bigger than day trades, so the same costs take a much smaller bite, and ten years covers several different markets. It places no orders.

How this is worked out. With a starting balance: one account replays every trade in date order and only takes a buy when it has the cash; profit or loss is counted when a trade is sold, so a position still open does not move the balance, and the daily return is the balance's change from one day to the next. Without one: every stock tested gets its own "slot" of the dollars-per-buy amount, so the strategy's money is (stocks tested × dollars per buy). Each day the open trades are valued at that day's close, and the day's profit or loss divided by that money is the strategy's return for the day. Days with nothing invested earn nothing (cash is assumed idle), and nothing is compounded. The S&P 500 is SPY's daily change over the same days, price only (no dividends, which the strategy does not collect either). "Average annual" is the average of the full calendar years in the window; "Compound growth" turns the whole window's total into a yearly rate. A strategy that is invested only part of the time can trail the index in total and still be worth having if it is much steadier, but it has to beat the index to be worth the effort on its own.

How the strategies and the test work

Buy sharp dips. If a stock closes above its 200-day average (a long-term uptrend) and its 2-day RSI is below 10 (it just dropped hard), the system buys at the next day's open. It sells at the open after the stock closes above its 5-day average, after 10 days at most, or at the stop.

Buy new highs. If a stock closes above its highest price of the previous 55 days (and above its 200-day average), it buys at the next open and holds until a close below the previous 20-day low, or the stop.

Stops and R. The stop sits a set number of average daily ranges (ATR) below the buy price, and that distance is 1R. If a day trades through the stop the test sells at the stop price, plus slippage; if a day opens below it, it sells at the open, which can be a loss bigger than 1R.

No peeking. Every signal uses only prices up to that day's close, and the order fills at the next day's open. The automated tests check that changing future prices never changes a trade that already happened.

Limits. Yahoo's daily prices are adjusted for stock splits but not dividends, so returns are slightly understated. The stock list is today's S&P 500, which leaves out companies that were removed after failing (survivorship bias) and flatters every long-only test, especially over ten years. Each stock is traded on its own with a fixed dollar amount, so this shows the strategy's edge, not the result of one real account with limited cash. Historical P/E ratios are not available from this data source (only today's), so valuation is not used here; testing it on past data would quietly use information that was unknown at the time.
